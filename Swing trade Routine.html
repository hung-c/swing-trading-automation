Quick Setup — Scans & Chart Templates (do this once)

Set these up once, then run them from the Daily Checklist below. Finviz runs the nightly discovery scan; Webull runs the charts, the trigger alerts, and the orders. The full reasoning for this split — and what to do if you would rather stay in one app — is under Scan A in the evening routine section further down.

Scan A — nightly watchlist (Finviz, free)

Screener → Filters → All tab. Set:

  • Price: Over $10
  • Market Cap: +Mid (over $2bln), or +Small for a wider net
  • Average Volume: Over 1M
  • 52-Week High/Low: 0-10% below High
  • Simple Moving Average: Price above SMA50
  • Simple Moving Average: Price above SMA200
  • Simple Moving Average: SMA50 above SMA200
  • Volatility: Week — Under 3%

Or paste this straight into the address bar:

https://finviz.com/screener.ashx?v=111&f=cap_midover,sh_avgvol_o1000,sh_price_o10,ta_highlow52w_b0to10h,ta_sma200_pa,ta_sma200_sb50,ta_sma50_pa,ta_volatility_wo3

Switch the view to Charts and set it to weekly — that turns the weekly skim into one scroll instead of forty tabs.

Add Earnings Date: Next 5 Days as a second, throwaway filter on the same screen to see which names to strike off tonight's list.

Scan B — intraday trigger (Webull price alerts)

With only 2–5 names, one alert per name beats any intraday scanner.

  1. Draw the resistance line on the daily chart during your evening review.
  1. Tap the price on the chart and set a price alert at that exact level.
  1. When it fires, confirm by eye: above yesterday's close, above VWAP, volume running hot for the time of day.

Webull has no volume-adjusted-for-time-of-day field, so that last check stays manual — compare current volume to what you would expect by this hour from the 50-day average.

Order execution (Webull bracket order)

Use a bracket order: entry limit at resistance + 0.5–1%, stop-loss at your planned stop, take-profit at your planned target, all placed at once. That way the stop exists the instant the entry fills, even if you step away from the screen.

Part 1 — Today's Routine. Run these six sections in order, every trading day.

Daily Checklist — the whole routine on one screen

Run this page. Everything below it is the explanation layer — open it when a step is unclear, not every day.

Evening — after the close (~20 minutes)

  • Run Scan A, the watchlist scan. Expect 20–80 names.
  • Weekly chart, 5–10 seconds per name. Keep only clean uptrends with no wall of overhead supply.
  • Daily chart on the survivors. Keep only the ones sitting in a 2–8 week base or flag.
  • Check the earnings date. Inside 7 trading days → drop it.
  • On each keeper: draw the resistance line and the stop level, then write down entry, stop, target, and share count.
  • Result: 2–5 names with a written plan. That is tomorrow's watchlist.

7:00–9:15 AM ET — Regime check (~5 minutes)

  • S&P 500 above its 50-day MA?
  • Nasdaq above its 50-day MA?
  • Futures not deeply red?
  • No high-impact release on the calendar today?
  • Set today's position size and max new entries from the regime scorecard.

9:30–10:00 AM — Do nothing

  • No entries in the first 30 minutes. Watch only.

10:00 AM–3:45 PM — Trigger and execute

  • Has any watchlist name traded through the resistance line I drew last night?
  • Is it above yesterday's close, above VWAP, and running above 1.5x its normal volume for this time of day?
  • If yes: buy at resistance +0.5% to 1%, and place the stop in the same minute.
  • If no: do nothing. A breakout you miss costs you zero.

3:45–4:00 PM — Manage

  • Any position closing back inside its base → exit, do not wait for the stop.
  • Any position at 2R → sell half, move the stop to breakeven.
  • Anything you are trailing → stop stays under the 8 EMA.

Hard rules

  • Never chase. More than 1% above the resistance line, the trade is gone. Wait for the next one.
  • Never hold a fresh breakout through an earnings report.
  • Never let total risk across all open positions exceed your heat cap.
  • Never widen a stop. Moving it further away is not risk management, it is hoping.

4:15–5:00 PM ET — After the close: scan and build tomorrow's watchlist

The pipeline starts here, the evening before you trade, not at 11 AM.

Reason: a breakout happens in a few minutes. If you are still discovering the stock while the breakout is running, you are already late, and you will end up chasing. Discovery and markup happen when the market is closed. During the session you only pull the trigger on work you already did.

Scan A — the watchlist scan

Purpose: find every stock that is capable of a breakout. Not one that is breaking out right now.

No intraday conditions in this scan. None. That is the whole point.

Liquidity and quality
  Last price               >  $10
  Market cap               >  $1,000,000,000
  Average volume (20 day)  >  1,000,000 shares

Trend
  Close                    >  200 SMA
  Close                    >  50 SMA
  50 SMA                   >  200 SMA

Position in the range
  Within 15% of the 52-week high

Tightness (pick whichever your scanner has)
  ATR(14) / price          <  5%
      or
  (10-day high - 10-day low) / price  <  10%

Expected output: 20–80 names. If you get 3, the market is weak and that is real information. If you get 400, tighten the 52-week-high and tightness filters.

Why each filter is there

  • $10 and $1B and 1M shares — liquidity. You need a tight spread and enough volume that your stop actually fills where you put it.
  • Above 200 SMA — the long-term trend is healthy. This filter alone removes most of the ways to lose money.
  • 50 SMA above 200 SMA — the medium-term trend is stronger than the long-term trend. The stock is accelerating, not decaying.
  • Within 15% of the 52-week high — stocks that break out to new highs have no trapped sellers above them. This is the filter you loosen first when the market is weak.
  • Tightness — a proxy for "is it resting?". A stock swinging 8% a day is not building a base, and its stop would be too far away to trade.

Field names on broker scanners

Most broker scanners have all of these, sometimes under different labels:

  • thinkorswim — Stock Hacker. The trend conditions go in as study filters: one SimpleMovingAvg filter for price versus length 200, a second for length 50, and ATR for tightness. Distance from the 52-week high is not a standard filter — add the 52-week-high column to the results table and sort by it instead.
  • IBKR — Market Scanner. Use "Price vs. SMA(200)" and "Price vs. SMA(50)" as percent-above filters, and "% below 52-week high".
  • Webull — covers the descriptive half (region, price, market cap, volume, turnover rate), but its technical section is a fixed list of preset signals: MACD golden cross, MA5 crossing above MA10, RSI states, candlestick patterns. There is no way to write "price above the 200-day SMA" or "50 SMA above 200 SMA". Webull cannot run Scan A on its own — see below.

When your broker's screener cannot express Scan A

Scan A depends on three conditions many broker screeners simply cannot state: price above the 200 SMA, price above the 50 SMA, and the 50 above the 200. Without them the scan degrades into "large liquid stocks", which is about 1,500 names and useless as a nightly list.

The fix is to split the tools: scan where the filters exist, trade where your money is.

Scan A   ->  Finviz free screener
Pass 1   ->  Finviz "Charts" view, switched to Weekly
Pass 2   ->  Webull daily chart, 8/21 EMA + 50/200 SMA + volume
Scan B   ->  Webull price alert on each resistance line
Order    ->  Webull bracket order

The exact Finviz filter values and a ready-to-paste URL are in Quick Setup — Scans & Chart Templates at the top of this page, so they only need maintaining in one place.

Two Finviz features that fit this routine unusually well. The Charts view renders every result as a thumbnail and can be switched to weekly, which turns Pass 1 into a single scroll instead of forty tabs. And the Earnings Date filter set to "Next 5 Days" gives you the exact list of names to delete from tonight's watchlist.

Everything after the scan happens in Webull. Its charting handles 8 EMA, 21 EMA, 50 SMA, 200 SMA and VWAP, which is all Pass 2 and Pass 3 need, and its price alerts are a better Scan B than any intraday screener.

If you would rather stay in one app, you can build the descriptive half of Scan A in Webull and save it — price over $10, market cap over $1B, volume over 1M. Just be honest about what that leaves you: roughly 1,500 results and the trend filtering done by eye, which nobody sustains nightly.

Scan B — the trigger scan

Purpose: tell you when a name you already marked up is actually going.

This scan runs against your watchlist only — typically 2 to 5 symbols — not the whole market.

Universe:  my watchlist only
  Price crossing above  the resistance line I drew
  Relative volume       > 1.5   (see the note below)
  Price                 > yesterday's close
  Price                 > VWAP

In practice, with 2–5 names, a price alert on each resistance line does the same job and is more reliable than an intraday scanner. Set the alert the night before, at the same time you draw the line.

Why the old scan returned zero results

The old scan asked for price > $10, market cap > $1B, average volume > 1M, today's volume > 1.5x average, today's gain > 3%, above the 50 and 200 SMA, and within 10% of the 52-week high — all at 11 AM, all at once. Two things were breaking it.

1. "Today's volume > 1.5x average" measured at 11 AM

Almost every scanner compares cumulative volume so far today against a full day's average. At 11 AM only about 40–50% of a normal session has traded. So asking for 1.5x a full day, half way through the morning, is really asking for about 3x normal pace.

Normal day, cumulative volume:

  9:30 - 10:30   ~30-35% of the day
 10:30 - 11:00   ~10%
        11:00    ~40-50% of the day is done

Asking for 1.5x full-day volume by 11:00
   =  ~3x the normal pace
   =  a gap-and-news stock, not a base breakout

On a typical day maybe five stocks in the entire market qualify, and they are gap-ups on news — the exact opposite of the quiet bases you want.

Fixes, best first:

  • Use a time-adjusted relative volume field if your scanner has one — it compares against the average volume by this hour, which is what you actually meant. Keep the threshold at 1.5.
  • If it only has the untimed version, set it to 0.7–0.9 for an 11 AM run. That is roughly the same thing.
  • Best of all: move the volume check out of the discovery scan entirely, which is what Scan A above does.

2. Filter stacking

Each filter is reasonable alone. ANDed together they multiply down to nothing.

US stocks                              ~6,000
  cap > $1B and avg vol > 1M       ~1,500-2,000
  + above 50 and 200 SMA               ~600-900
  + within 10% of 52-week high         ~150-400   (near 0 in a correction)
  + up 3% today                           ~5-30
  + 1.5x volume at 11 AM                    0-3

What was not the problem

The $10 price floor. Nearly every company worth over $1B trades above $10, so that filter is removing almost nothing. Removing it will not bring back results. Keep it.

The "today's gain 3%" filter is deleted

It contradicts the first rule of the entry section. Screening for stocks that already moved 3%, then buying them above resistance an hour later, is chasing. You want to be there before the 3%, not after it.

Loosening ladder — when the scan still returns nothing

Loosen in this order, one step at a time, and stop as soon as you have 20 names:

  1. 52-week high proximity: 15% → 20% → 25%
  1. Tightness: ATR 5% → 7%, or drop it and judge by eye
  1. Average volume: 1M → 500k (only if you are trading small size)
  1. Market cap: $1B → $500M

Never loosen these — they are the strategy, not a preference:

  • Close above the 200 SMA
  • 50 SMA above the 200 SMA

If you have gone through the whole ladder and still have under 10 names above the 200 SMA, the answer is not a looser scan. The answer is that breakouts are not working right now. Sit out.

Mark up the survivors

Run the scan output through the chart skim order below (weekly, then daily). For each name that survives, write the plan down before you close the laptop:

Ticker:        ABCD
Pattern:       flat base, 6 weeks
Resistance:    $110.00     <- horizontal line drawn on the daily
Entry:         $110.55     (resistance + 0.5%)
Stop:          $103.50     (below the base low at $104)
Risk/share:    $7.05
Shares:        (dollar risk) / 7.05
Target 1:      $124.65     (2R)
Earnings:      Nov 12      (18 trading days away - clear)
Alert set:     yes, at $110.00

A name without a written plan is not on the watchlist. If you have not decided the stop before the open, you will decide it during the breakout, and you will decide it wrong.

7:00–9:15 AM ET — Market regime check

Five minutes. This does not pick stocks. It sets your position size for the day, and occasionally tells you not to trade at all.

Is the S&P 500 above its 50-day MA?

Daily chart of SPX or SPY.

The S&P 500 represents the overall market, and most stocks follow the market. A good setup in a bad tape still fails.

✅ Above the 50 SMA → breakouts work, trade normally.

⚠️ Below → cut your size and take fewer trades.

Is the Nasdaq above its 50-day MA?

Daily chart of IXIC or QQQ.

Your breakout candidates are mostly technology and growth names, so the Nasdaq is the more honest read on whether your kind of stock is healthy. When the S&P holds up but the Nasdaq is under its 50-day, that usually means money is rotating into defensives and your setups will keep failing.

Are futures green or red?

Futures trade overnight and show whether traders are buying or selling before the bell.

ES1!   S&P 500 futures
NQ1!   Nasdaq futures

Chart: 15 minute. No indicators. Just the % change.
  • Flat to green → nothing to do, proceed.
  • Worse than −0.75% → no entries before 10:30. Gap-down mornings reverse hard in both directions.

Futures only affect the first hour of the day. Do not let a red pre-market cancel a breakout that triggers at 2 PM.

Any high-impact news today?

A perfect setup can fail purely because of a macro surprise. You buy the NVDA breakout at +5%, the Fed surprises on rates an hour later, the whole market drops, your stop is hit, and nothing was wrong with your analysis.

Check any economic calendar for high impact US events only:

CPI
Fed interest rate decision (FOMC)
Jobs report (NFP)
PPI
GDP
  • Release at 8:30 AM → no new entries before 10:00.
  • FOMC at 2:00 PM → no new entries after 1:30. The move before the announcement is meaningless.

Regime scorecard — what today's answers mean

MarketPosition sizeMax new entries today

One above, one belowHalf2

Either index below its 200-day MANone0 — stand aside and keep scanning

Keep scanning every evening even when you are standing aside. The names that build bases during a correction are the ones that lead when it ends.

9:30–10:00 AM — Do nothing

No entries in the first 30 minutes.

The open is the widest spreads and the most fake moves of the day: overnight orders clearing, gaps filling, algorithms probing both sides. A "breakout" at 9:32 on a one-minute burst reverses more often than it continues.

You are not missing anything. A real breakout is still above the line at 10:15, and by then you can see whether the volume is real.

Use the half hour to re-read your written plans and confirm your alerts are set.

10:00 AM–3:45 PM — Trigger and execute

You are not looking for new ideas during the session. You are only checking whether last night's plans triggered.

The three intraday confirmations

Chart: 15-minute, with VWAP and a horizontal line at yesterday's close. All three must be true.

1. Is it making higher highs today?

Each new peak higher than the last, each dip shallower than the last.

Strong                    Weak
 9:30   $100               $100
10:00   $102               $102
10:30   $101               $ 99
11:00   $105               $101
11:30   $104               $ 98
12:00   $108
        higher highs       no direction

2. Is it above yesterday's close?

Draw a horizontal line at yesterday's closing price. Price above it means today's buyers are still in control of the gains. Price sliding back under it means the move is being sold.

    Today
55   *
     *
50   ---------------- yesterday's close
     *

Above the line = keep it. Below = drop it for today.

3. Is the volume real?

A breakout without volume is the most expensive thing on this page. Volume answers one question: are institutions participating, or is this a few retail orders drifting the price up?

Add a relative volume indicator to the chart and read it against the time of day:

Time-adjusted RVOL  > 1.5   good
                    > 2.0   excellent

Or by hand:
  average daily volume     2,000,000
  by 11 AM you'd expect     ~900,000  (~45% of a day)
  today by 11 AM          1,500,000   = strong

Price      /\            Price      /
        __/                      __/
Volume ||||||||||||     Volume  ||
       ||||||||||||             ||
        real                    fake

VWAP is the quick version of the same idea: a stock that stays above VWAP all day is being accumulated, one that keeps sinking under it is being distributed.

Placing the trade

  1. Buy at the entry price from your written plan — resistance plus 0.5% to 1%. Not higher.
  1. Place the stop order in the same minute, at the level from your plan. Not later, not "once it settles down".
  1. Use the share count from your plan. Do not re-size in the moment because it "looks strong".

If you cannot watch the screen, put in a stop-limit buy at the entry with an attached stop-loss — a bracket order — the night before. That mechanically enforces the no-chasing rule, because a limit will simply not fill if the stock gaps past it.

If it is already more than 1% above the line by the time you look: it is gone. Let it go. There will be another one next week, and stocks bought late are the ones that stop you out.

3:45–4:00 PM — Manage what you hold

Your hard stop orders sit in the market all day and protect you without supervision. This 15 minutes is for the decisions a stop order cannot make.

  • Failed breakout — price closing back inside the base it broke out of, on the same day or the next. Exit now at the close, do not wait for the stop. The reason you bought is gone, so the position should be too.
  • At 2R — sell half, move the stop on the rest to breakeven. Now the trade cannot lose.
  • Trailing a winner — keep the stop just under the 8 EMA and raise it as the 8 EMA rises. Never lower it.
  • Earnings inside 2 days — decide now: close it, or halve it and accept the gap risk on the rest. Do not arrive at the report undecided.

Part 2 — Reference. Open a section below only when a step in Part 1 needs explaining, or when you are studying the strategy — not every trading day.

Which chart to open first — the skim order

You have 20–80 names from Scan A and maybe 3 of them deserve real work. The first pass exists to reject fast. Spending five minutes on every name is how this routine gets abandoned in week two.

PassChartBars on screenTime eachThe one question
1Weekly2–3 years5–10 secondsReal uptrend, and is there clear air above?
2Daily6–9 months3–5 minutesIs there a base, and where are entry and stop?
315-minute or hourly5–10 days1 minuteIs it triggering today?
—Monthly5–10 yearsOnce a quarterIs this a long-term winner at all?

Pass 1 — Weekly, 2 to 3 years, 5 to 10 seconds

Start here, not on the monthly. Two reasons.

You hold for three to six weeks. On a monthly chart that entire trade is one candle, still forming — it has almost no power to reject a name, so it is a slow first filter. The weekly, by contrast, puts a full year on one screen and answers the stage question immediately.

More importantly, the weekly is the only place you can see overhead supply: old price levels where buyers got trapped and are waiting to sell at breakeven. That is the most common reason a technically perfect breakout fails, and it is invisible on a 6-month daily chart.

CLEAR AIR - what you want

                        /\   <- new high. Nobody above you is
                      /         underwater and waiting to sell.
              /\    /
            /    \/
    /\    /
  /    \/


OVERHEAD SUPPLY - avoid

$120  ****    <- heavy trading here, then it crashed.
          \      Everyone who bought is waiting to get
            \    out at breakeven.
              \      /\
                \  /    <- you are here at $110. Your breakout
                 \/        has to chew through all of that
                           supply before it can go anywhere.

Four things, five seconds:

  • Is it up and to the right?
  • Is price above a rising 30-week (150-day) or 50-week MA? Flat or falling is not good enough.
  • Is it near the top of its one-year range?
  • Is there clear air above, or a wall of old highs sitting 8% overhead?

Any "no" → close the tab, next name. Do not go looking for reasons to keep it.

Pass 2 — Daily, 6 to 9 months, 3 to 5 minutes

This is where 80% of the work belongs, on the handful of names that survived the weekly. Indicators: 8 EMA, 21 EMA, 50 SMA, 200 SMA, volume.

  1. Score the trend template below. Under 6 of 8 → drop it.
  1. Name the pattern and measure it against the lookback windows.
  1. Draw the resistance line. Write the exact price down.
  1. Find the stop, using the priority order in the stop-loss section.
  1. Do the risk/reward arithmetic. Under 2:1 → drop it, however good the chart looks.

Step 5 is the one people skip. A beautiful setup with the stop 12% away and the next resistance 6% up is a bad trade, and no amount of pattern quality fixes that.

Pass 3 — 15-minute or hourly, 5 to 10 days, 1 minute

Only for the 2–5 finalists, and only on a day one of them is actually near its line. This is the trigger check from the 10:00 AM–3:45 PM section: higher highs, above yesterday's close, above VWAP, volume running hot for the time of day.

Nothing on this timeframe can turn a bad daily setup into a good trade. It only tells you when, never whether.

Monthly — once a quarter, not every day

The monthly chart answers "is this a long-term winner?", which is worth knowing but does not change from Tuesday to Wednesday. Reviewing it nightly costs you 30 seconds a name for information you already had. Do it once a quarter over your recurring names instead.

Setup rules — the single source of truth

Every rule in this routine is defined here, once. The time-of-day sections above tell you when to apply them; this section is what they mean.

The trend template — score it out of 8

Daily chart. 6 or better makes the watchlist. Under 6 is dropped, no exceptions and no negotiating with the chart.

QuestionYesNo
1. Price above the 200 SMA?☐☐
2. 50 SMA above the 200 SMA?☐☐
3. 8 EMA above the 21 EMA?☐☐
4. Price above the 8 EMA?☐☐
5. Making higher highs?☐☐
6. Making higher lows?☐☐
7. Volume drying up during the base?☐☐
8. Within ~10% of the 52-week high?☐☐

What each one is actually telling you:

  1. Price above the 200 SMA — the long-term trend is healthy. Below it you are trying to catch a falling stock, which is a different game with worse odds.
  1. 50 SMA above the 200 SMA — the medium-term trend is stronger than the long-term one. The stock is speeding up rather than decaying.
  1. 8 EMA above the 21 EMA — short-term momentum is positive right now.
  1. Price above the 8 EMA — if it keeps closing below the 8 EMA, momentum is draining out of it.
  1. Higher highs — each new peak clears the previous peak.
  1. Higher lows — each pullback stops above the previous pullback. Buyers are getting more eager, not less.
  1. Volume drying up in the base — see below. Sellers are finished.
  1. Near the 52-week high — within about 5–10% means nobody above you is underwater and waiting to sell.

Note that 1 and 2 measure related things, so a stock in a long uptrend gets both almost for free. A score of 6 that is missing 5, 6 and 7 is much weaker than a 6 missing 1 and 8 — read the misses, do not just add up the checkmarks.

What a good base looks like

A base is the resting area before the breakout: the stock stops going up and moves sideways while it changes hands from short-term traders to longer-term holders.

GOOD - a base                    BAD - not a base
110                              100
108                              130
106     ______                    90
108    |      |                  140
109    |      |
107 ___|      |___               too unstable, and the stop
110                              would have to be 25% away

tight range, small candles
  • Length — 2 to 8 weeks. Under 2 weeks it has not rested; over about 12 weeks the momentum is gone.
  • Depth — high to low under 15%. Under 10% is excellent. A "base" that drops 30% is a downtrend with a nice name.
  • Tightening — the best bases get quieter toward the right edge, each swing smaller than the last.
  • A prior advance — there must have been a real move up into the base, 25% or more over the previous 3–12 months. Without it you are not buying a pause in an uptrend, you are buying a bottom, which is a lower-probability trade with a different playbook.
  • Base count — the first and second base after a stock emerges from a downtrend work best. By the fourth or fifth base of a long run, everyone can see it and failures increase.

Volume dry-up, and the breakout expansion

Inside the base you want volume to shrink. Falling volume while price holds means the sellers have finished and there is nobody left to push it down. Then on the breakout day you want it to explode, because that is institutions stepping in.

Price   110 ________ 108 ________ 106 ________      then  /
                                                        /
Volume  ||||||||                                        |||||||||||||||||
        |||||                                           |||||||||||||||||
        ||||
        ||          <- drying up: nobody selling         <- 1.5-2x average

BAD - heavy volume all through the base:
        ||||||||||||||||||
        ||||||||||||||||||   someone big is distributing into the range
        ||||||||||||||||||   avoid

Compare against the 50-day average volume. A breakout on below-average volume is the single most reliable way to lose money with this strategy.

Overhead supply

Every price level where the stock traded heavily and then fell is a queue of buyers who are underwater. When price returns to their entry, a lot of them sell just to get out flat. That selling is what kills breakouts.

How to check: weekly chart, 12 to 24 months back. Look for old highs and heavy-volume shelves above your entry. If there is a big one within 10% of your target, either the target moves down or the trade goes in the bin.

This is exactly why "near the 52-week high" is in the scan. At a new high there is nobody above you.

Patterns — what you are looking for, and how far back to look

All four patterns are found on the daily chart. That is the timeframe where a 2–8 week base is 10 to 40 candles: enough resolution to place a resistance line to the cent and to see exactly where the last swing low sits.

Not the weekly — the same six-week base is six candles there, which is context, not a tradable level. Not the 15-minute — the pattern dissolves into noise.

Flat base and bull flag are the two to learn first. They are the easiest to identify and they fit the 8 EMA plus 200 SMA breakout approach best.

The row people skip is the prior advance. Sideways price with no run into it is a bottom, not a base.

How to sort your scan output into patterns

Open each weekly-pass survivor on a 6-month daily and ask these in order. The first "yes" names the pattern; you do not need to keep going.

  1. Did it run hard in the last 1–4 weeks and is now drifting sideways or slightly down? → bull flag
  1. Has it been sideways in a tight range for 5–8 weeks, after an earlier advance? → flat base
  1. Is the top flat while the lows keep rising? → ascending triangle
  1. Is there a U-shape over the last 2–6 months with a small pullback at the right edge? → cup and handle
  1. None of the above → there is no setup here today. Close it. It may well be back next week.

Rough expectation, starting from 40 scan results:

40  scan results
25  survive the weekly skim
10  are resting in something base-like
 5  are clean enough to name the pattern
 2  survive the risk/reward arithmetic   <- these get a written plan

Two a night is normal and healthy. Some nights it is zero. That is the routine working, not failing.

Kill it regardless of how good the shape is if any of these are true:

  • Earnings within 7 trading days.
  • The resistance line is more than about 8% above the current price — too far away to be a trade you take tomorrow. Put it on a "watch later" list instead.
  • The only sensible stop is more than 8% below the entry and you cannot size down enough to make the dollar risk work.
  • Heavy overhead supply between the entry and the 2R target.

Bull Flag ⭐⭐⭐⭐

A stock runs up hard — the flag pole — then rests sideways or drifts slightly down.

Price
  |
  |          *
  |         *
  |        *          ______
  |       *          /      \
  |      *          /        \____
  |     *      ____/               ^
  |    *                        breakout
  |___*
       flag pole            the flag

How to find it on the daily:

  1. The pole — a strong move, say $100 to $120, over a few days to a few weeks.
  1. The flag — 5 to 20 trading days of sideways drift, pulling back less than 10–15% off the high.
  1. The line — draw resistance across the top of the flag.

Stop goes below the flag low. On a $120 flag with the low at $115, entry $120.50 and stop $114.50 is $6 of risk per share.

Flat Base ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

The best pattern to start with. The stock simply goes quiet in a narrow range.

previous move: $80 -> $110, then:

$110 ----------------  <- resistance
       110  109  110
       108  107  108
       106  105  107
$105 ----------------  <- base low

5-8 weeks. This range is 4.8% deep - well under the 15% limit.

Draw the line across the highest closes in the range, not the single highest wick — one spike that nobody followed is not where the sellers are.

Stop goes below the base low. The base low is the cleanest stop level in this whole strategy, which is a large part of why this pattern rates highest.

Ascending Triangle ⭐⭐⭐

A flat top with rising lows. Buyers are getting more aggressive while the sellers hold one price.

$120 ----------------  <- flat resistance, sellers here
                  /
$115            /
              /
$110        /
          /
$105    /               <- lows keep rising

Sellers keep offering at $120. Buyers step up at $105, then $110, then $115. Eventually the sellers run out and the price goes through.

Stop goes below the most recent higher low — not the bottom of the whole triangle, which is far too far away by the time the pattern matures.

Cup and Handle ⭐⭐⭐

The longest of the four. Zoom the daily out to 6–12 months or you will not see it.

$100 ----------------  <- resistance (the old high)

        ______
      /        \                  |____
    /            \                     \
   /              \__                   \____  <- handle
  /                  \
                      \__
                      the cup                  ^ breakout
  • Cup — falls from $100 to $80, recovers to $100. A rounded U, not a sharp V. 7 weeks to 6 months or more.
  • Handle — a small pullback near the old high, 1 to 3 weeks, ideally in the upper half of the cup.

Stop goes below the handle low, never below the cup. Note that the old high at the top of the cup is overhead supply by definition, which is why this pattern rates below the flat base despite its reputation.

Entry, stop, size and target — the trade plan

Entry formula — one rule for all four patterns

Never chase candles. Wait for price to break the resistance line you drew last night.

Entry = resistance + 0.5% to 1%

Resistance $24.80   ->  buy $24.90 - $25.00
                        NOT $26.50 after it already ran

The small premium above the line is the price of confirmation. Buying exactly at the line gets you filled on wicks that poke through and immediately fail; paying half a percent more means the level actually gave way.

The breakout must come with expanding volume — 1.5 to 2 times the 50-day average. Without that, skip it, whatever the price is doing.

This formula does not change between bull flags, flat bases, triangles or cups. Only the location of the line changes.

Stop loss — always below structure

Structure decides the level. A percentage never does. Work down this priority list and take the first one that exists:

  1. Below the consolidation low — best. Entry $100, base low $94, stop $93.50. If price is back under the base, every buyer in it is wrong, including you.
  1. Below a support level — entry $100, support $95, stop $94.50.
  1. Below the breakout candle low — entry $53 on a candle that ran $50 to $55, stop $49.50. Tightest, and the most likely to be hit by noise.

What support actually is

A price area where buyers have repeatedly stepped in. Find it on the daily chart, looking back 1 to 3 months, at places where price stopped falling and bounced more than once.

$120  *
       *
$110 ----------------  resistance
       \      /\
        \    /  \
$100 ------*------*---  support   <- buyers defend here twice

The 8% rule — how far is too far

Structure gives you the level; then you measure the distance and decide whether the trade is takeable at all.

stop is  under  8% away  ->  normal trade, size normally
stop is  8-12%  away     ->  only if you can cut share count enough
                             that the dollar risk still fits.
                             With a small account, usually you cannot.
                             Then: skip it.
stop is  over  12% away  ->  skip. The base is too loose to trade.

This replaces the old "5–8% below entry" guideline, which conflicted with "below pattern support" every time the two disagreed. They no longer disagree: support sets the price, the percentage only decides whether you take the trade.

The test

Ask of any candidate stop: "if price reaches this level, is my breakout idea wrong?" If yes, it is a good stop. If the idea would still be intact there, the stop is too tight and noise will take you out.

And then: never move it further away. Widening a stop converts a planned small loss into an unplanned large one, and it is the single most expensive habit in trading.

Position size

Shares = dollar risk ÷ (entry − stop)

With a $100 account risking 3%, the dollar risk is $3:

EntryStopRisk per shareShares
$20.00$19.00$1.003
$20.00$19.50$0.506

Look at the second row: 6 shares at $20 is $120, which is more than the whole account, so in reality you would buy 5. With a small account you are often limited by what you can afford rather than by the risk formula, and your real risk ends up below the maximum. That is fine — it is the safe direction to be wrong in.

A note on 3%

3% per trade is aggressive. The conventional range is 0.5% to 1%, and there is a reason: six losses in a row is completely normal in this strategy, and at 3% that is 18% of the account gone, which needs a 22% gain to recover.

On $100 the dollar amounts are trivial and 3% is a reasonable way to get enough shares to make trades meaningful. But treat it as a starting-out allowance, not a permanent rule. Somewhere around $2,000–5,000, step it down to 1%.

Portfolio heat — the cap on total risk

Risk per trade is not the same thing as risk overall. Six positions at 3% each is an 18% loss if the market turns and they all stop out together — and in a real selloff they do all stop out together.

Portfolio heat = sum of (entry − stop) × shares, across all open positions

Cap:                   6% of the account
Max open positions:    4 to 6
  • At 3% per trade the cap means two positions at full risk. A third has to wait for one of the first two to move its stop up.
  • Once a stop is at breakeven that position's risk is zero and it stops counting against the cap. This is a concrete reason to move to breakeven at 2R, beyond how it feels.
  • Correlation counts. Three semiconductor names are one trade wearing three tickers. Count a cluster in the same industry as a single position against the cap.

Earnings — check it before every single entry

Look up the confirmed next earnings date before you commit to any trade. This takes ten seconds and prevents the worst losses you will ever take.

  • No new entry within 7 trading days of the report.
  • Already holding when earnings arrive? Decide in advance: close it, or cut to half and accept the gap risk on the rest.

The reason this matters more than it sounds: a stop order does not protect you against a gap. The stock can open 20% below your stop and you will be filled there, at the open, on the way down. Everything else in this routine assumes your loss is capped at the stop. Earnings is the one event that breaks that assumption.

An earnings gap is close to a coin flip regardless of how perfect the chart looks. That is not a swing trade, it is a bet, and it is not what this routine is for.

Target price

Nobody can predict where a stock will stop. You are not predicting, you are setting a level that makes the arithmetic work.

Method 1 — R multiples (use this one)

Entry   $20
Stop    $19
Risk    $1     = 1R

Target  $22    = 2R    minimum
Target  $23    = 3R    better

Under 2R, the trade does not get taken. With a 2R target you can be wrong more often than right and still make money; at 1R you need to be right about two thirds of the time, which you will not be.

Method 2 — measured move (a sanity check on Method 1)

Measure the height of the base and project it up from the breakout point.

Base high    $50
Base low     $46
Height       $4

Breakout at  $50   ->  measured target  $54

Now check it against your risk:
Entry  $50.25
Stop   $45.50   (below the base low)
Risk   $4.75
$54 target = 0.8R   ->  REJECT

That example fails on purpose. When the measured move comes out below 2R, the base is too deep for its own height and the trade is not worth taking. Use Method 2 to sanity-check Method 1, never as a target on its own.

Method 3 — ride the trend

No fixed target. Stay in until price closes below the 8 EMA, or breaks down on heavy volume. This is how the handful of trades that pay for the whole year happen — a fixed target guarantees you are not in one of them.

Taking profit

  • Option 1 (default) — sell 50% at 2R, move the stop to breakeven, trail the rest under the 8 EMA. Locks in a win, frees up portfolio heat, and still leaves you in if it runs.
  • Option 2 — sell everything at 3R. Simple and mechanical, good while you are learning.
  • Option 3 — pure trend ride, exit only on a close below the 8 EMA.

Pick one and use it for 20 trades before you change it. Switching method mid-trade is how people end up selling their winners early and holding their losers late.

Separate from all of this: if price closes back inside the base after breaking out, exit immediately regardless of which option you are running. The setup failed; there is nothing left to manage.

Position size — where a $100 account meets reality

You cannot buy 0.41 of a share at most brokers, and one whole share would put $118 — more than the entire account — at stake. So on a $100 account this trade is simply not available. Two honest options:

  • Use a broker with fractional shares, and take the 0.41.
  • Or restrict yourself to $10–$30 names, where one share is a sensible unit. Same setup at entry $22.40 with a $1.10 stop distance gives you 2 shares and a real position.

This is not a flaw in the strategy, it is what a $100 account is. The routine still works; the pool of tradable names is just smaller until the account grows.
